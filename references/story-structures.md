# Story Structures: Dramaturgical Containers (Ch. 23)

The story comes from the USER — this file never replaces it. It exists for the moment after intake: the user has a story seed, and it needs a dramaturgical container before treatment work starts (production-pipeline ch. 2). Same seed, different container, different film: a Simpsons episode, a Python film, Star Wars, and a Tarantino picture could all start from "someone loses everything over a small lie" and land in four different worlds. Offer containers like recipes: max 2 candidates, one line each, the user picks.

## 23a. Story intake gate (run BEFORE offering structures)

Check the user's story for filmable completeness — a lint, not a writing seminar:
protagonist + concrete want? · engine (conflict, question, or tension that pulls forward)? · a turn (something changes irreversibly)? · an ending (even a direction)? · whose POV? · fits the target length?
Gaps → targeted questions, one at a time. Never develop story unasked; when the user asks for help on a gap, propose 2–3 options and let them choose. A complete-enough story proceeds to container choice; structure gaps found later cost rerolls, not rewrites.

## 23b. Classic containers

**Hollywood three-act (Syd Field's paradigm)** — Shape: setup (world + want, inciting incident ~10–15%) → **Plot Point I** (the event that flings into act 2) → confrontation (rising obstacles, midpoint reversal, all-is-lost) → **Plot Point II** (triggers the ending — Wilder's second-act curtain) → resolution (climax, new equilibrium). Beat-sheet refinements (Save-the-Cat-style percentages) are optional scaffolding on top. Fits: the default for narrative 5 min+; audiences parse it without effort. Signals: clear goal, clear opposition. AI note: act structure maps 1:1 onto sequence planning; the midpoint reversal is the natural place for a look/palette shift (film-craft color arc).

**Hero's journey (monomyth)** — Shape: departure (ordinary world, call, refusal, mentor, threshold) → initiation (trials, ordeal, reward) → return (transformed, elixir). Star Wars is the canonical execution. Fits: myth, adventure, fantasy, transformation arcs, family animation (want-vs-need maps onto it). Signals: a world to cross into; a protagonist who must change. AI note: the threshold crossings are your biggest set-piece/look changes — budget new location assets per world, not per scene.

**Five-act (Freytag/Shakespeare)** — Shape: exposition → rising action → climax at the CENTER → falling action → catastrophe/denouement. Fits: tragedies, rise-AND-fall arcs (Scorsese pairing), prestige-drama episodes. Signals: the fall is the point; the peak belongs in the middle. AI note: the long back half needs its own visual escalation plan — decay dramaturgy (grade, light) carries what plot repetition would bore.

## 23c. Alternative containers

**Kishōtenketsu (ki–shō–ten–ketsu)** — Shape: introduction → development (deepening, no escalating conflict) → TWIST (a recontextualizing element, not an antagonist) → reconciliation. The East Asian four-act; Ghibli's slice-of-life films live here (Kiki: vignettes of settling in, then the crisis-twist, then quiet resolution). Fits: quiet wonder, slice-of-life, family, essay-adjacent fiction. Signals: no villain, no race — the story wants to breathe. AI note: a gift for generation — calm held shots, "ma" beats, no action chains; pair with Miyazaki/Ozu recipes.

**Nonlinear / chapter structure** — Shape: story order ≠ plot order; chapters or interleaved timelines whose MEANING assembles at the end (Tarantino: shuffled chapters; Nolan: parallel lines converging). Fits: crime, puzzles, ensemble webs. Signals: the reveal of HOW pieces connect is the payoff. AI note: generation order is free anyway — the risk is continuity per timeline (ledger mandatory, post-audio-legal ch. 19); label every shot with its timeline; the audience needs one anchor cue per timeline (palette, ratio, or grain — style-control era looks).

**Mosaic / fragment (associative)** — Shape: moments, not scenes, linked by emotional association and voice-over; time dissolves (Malick). Fits: memory, grief, love-and-loss, poetic essay. Signals: the user's material is feelings and images more than events. AI note: the most generation-friendly container of all — short independent clips, no continuity chains, VO carries coherence (no lip-sync).

**Circular / bookend** — Shape: ending returns to the opening image/scene with transformed meaning. Cheap to build, strong closure; combines with any container above. AI note: generate opening and closing from the SAME anchor still with changed light/grade — a controlled, prompt-friendly rhyme.

**Vignette / anthology** — Shape: self-contained segments under one theme, world, or object; optional frame story; runners/callbacks stitch. Fits: omnibus shorts, sketch features, anthology horror. AI note: resets continuity per segment — ideal when asset drift risk is high; each segment can even own a different recipe (declare it, don't drift into it).

**A24 / elevated-genre dramaturgy** 🟡 — critic-distilled, not an official studio doc; the studio's actual "concept" is business-side (director-driven, low budget, creative control, strangeness preserved — never sand it down). The narrative pattern across its catalog: a GENRE CONTAINER carries a psychological core as metaphor (possession film = inheritance of family trauma; folk horror = grief seeking a container); slow-burn pacing with anticipatory dread instead of jump-beats; tone/vibe as the organizing principle over plot mechanics; exposition near zero — interiority externalized in images and behavior (show-don't-tell as doctrine); tonal blends licensed (comedy inside horror, dread inside romance); endings resolve the EMOTION, not necessarily the plot — ambiguity is allowed to stand. Fits: psychological horror/drama, festival-style shorts, auteur pieces. Signals: the user names a feeling or wound first and a genre second. AI note: slow-burn = long held shots (extension chains, reroll budget); the metaphor layer lives in production design and recurring motifs — put the motif objects into the asset order from day one; pair with Kubrick/Lynch/Villeneuve structures + Young/Fraser light.

## 23d. Format-specific containers

**Sitcom episode (Simpsons shape)** — Shape: cold-open gag (often unrelated) → act 1 establishes a premise that PIVOTS into the real story (the famous misdirect: the episode is never about how it starts) → A-plot braided with B-plot (C optional) → escalation → resolution that RESETS the status quo. ~22 min native, scales down. Fits: episodic comedy, animated series. AI note: the reset is an asset strategy — recurring sets/cast amortize across episodes (production bible ch. 22a rule 6); the act-1 pivot licenses a mid-episode location/look change.

**Series formats (TV & YouTube): episodic universe vs. serialized film** — decide the pole FIRST; it is the most consequential format decision a series makes:
- **Episodic (stable universe):** every episode self-contained; the status quo resets; the universe changes at most at SEASON boundaries. Each episode carries its own complete container (sitcom shape, case-of-the-week three-act); viewers can enter anywhere. AI note: the economically ideal series form — cast sheets, sets, and trained identities amortize across episodes while the continuity risk RESETS per episode; universe changes are season-boundary decisions, recorded in the project bible (ch. 22a rule 6).
- **Serialized (the overlong film):** one continuous arc chopped into episodes — plan it AS a film first (any 23b/23c container), then cut episode boundaries at reveal/reversal points; each episode still needs its own micro-shape: entry hook (or recap device), internal rise, an OUT (cliffhanger or resonance beat). Viewers must enter at episode 1. AI note: the hardest continuity class — wardrobe, props, injuries, time-of-day carry ACROSS episodes; shot board and continuity ledger span the whole season (one bible, no per-episode reset). Generation order stays free — placement is fixed in the paper plan, exactly like scattered soundbites.
- **The hybrid (standard TV pattern):** episodic A-plot + a serialized B-arc that surfaces in a few beats per episode and takes over in finales; the B-arc is a season-level container (usually a reveal ladder) — track its beats in the bible or they drift.
- **Anthology series (the third pole):** every episode its own story, cast, even look — the universe is a THEME, tone, or frame device (the brand is the continuity). Each episode picks its own container and may pick its own recipe; only the frame device, title grammar, and one tonal contract persist. AI note: zero cross-episode continuity burden AND full per-episode asset freedom — the format where AI series production is cheapest per minute; the bible shrinks to the tonal contract + frame device. A **limited series** is simply the serialized pole with a fixed ending — plan it strictly as one film, never leave the arc open.
- **YouTube series:** the episode is a retention object — hook inside the first 15–30 seconds (no slow cold open unless the channel brand carries it), typical 8–15-min bodies, an out that hands off to the next episode (end-screen logic). Branding — intro, framing, palette, the channel's style anchor — IS the universe marker. Episodic entries win discovery (the platform serves single episodes to new viewers); serialized needs a recap device for enter-anywhere viewers. Title/thumbnail promise is decided at SCRIPT stage, and the episode must pay it — an unpaid thumbnail is a broken contract, not marketing.

**Sketch logic (Python shape)** — Shape: one absurd premise escalated relentlessly; NO resolution owed — exit at the peak, interrupt, or break the form itself (abrupt endings, intertitles, animation inserts). A sketch FILM is anthology + runners/callbacks wearing a loose quest frame. Fits: absurdist comedy, sketch features. Signals: the joke is the engine; a plot would slow it down. AI note: form-breaks legitimize model discontinuity (the ZAZ/Brooks recipe's Python variant is the visual grammar); every sketch is a fresh generation context — cheap.

**Short-film dramaturgy** — Shape: ONE situation, ONE turn; enter at the latest possible moment, leave at the earliest; ending lands as sting, reversal, or image-answer. Under ~3 min: a single reversal is the whole story — a compressed three-act is a feature synopsis, not a short. Fits: social clips, festival shorts, spec pieces. AI note: the turn shot deserves the reroll budget; everything else is economy.

**Documentary spines** (argument structure — interview production mechanics in ch. 7b, look defaults in genre-baselines) — Pick ONE: essay/thesis (claim unfolded in chapters — Wenders/Malick tone), chronological/biographical (era grammar as chapters), investigative reveal ladder (staged revelations, true-crime pacing — withhold like a thriller), observational arc (vérité: a situation ripens on its own clock), portrait mosaic (facets, not timeline). AI note: the spine decides the edit BEFORE generation — B-roll is generated to prove the narration, so lock narration first (audio-first, ch. 18 logic); disclosure rules ch. 20 apply throughout.

**Interview dramaturgy** (the layer between spine and staging — production mechanics in ch. 7b) — Five working rules: **(1) Paper edit first:** in AI production the transcript IS the script — every soundbite is written before anything is generated (audio-first), so the film's argument is built at transcript level, not discovered in the edit bay; write answers AS soundbites (one thought each, cuttable at both ends). **Cut order ≠ interview order:** a good interview is never obliged to run chronologically as conducted — soundbites are placement-free building blocks, scattered across acts wherever the argument needs them (the "last" answer may open the film), and the emotional arc (composed → cracking → silence) is AUTHORED from bites, not inherited from a conversation. Two crafts make that work: phrase every bite self-contained (the question folded into the answer, no "as I said before", no dangling referents), and assign each bite its film position in the paper edit BEFORE generation. **(2) Voice functions:** cast interviewees like characters — witness (was there), expert (explains), adversary (holds the counter-position), heart (the emotional center); every voice earns its screen time through its function, and no voice serves two functions in the same act. **(3) The conflict engine:** contradiction editing — opposing answers cut against each other CREATE the debate (opposing look sides, ch. 7b, make it spatial); conflicting accounts of one event are the doc's Rashomon container. **(4) Hook and reveal placement:** cold-open on the strongest soundbite as the film's promise; withhold the key witness or the decisive answer the way a thriller withholds the monster (reveal ladder); Mamet's three questions apply to interview segments too — a segment no one needs RIGHT NOW is B-roll fodder, not an act beat. **(5) The held silence:** the emotional peak of an interview is the pause, the swallow, the look away — hold the face and do NOT cut to B-roll there; it is the one moment the B-roll escape is forbidden (and the shot that earns the reroll budget, ch. 7b).

**Online-native short formats** (where AI generation is used heaviest) — four working shapes, all governed by retention dramaturgy: hook inside 0–3 s (visual question or mid-action entry — never an establishing shot), re-hook every 20–30 s (new open loop before the last one closes), payoff that answers the opening promise, loop-friendly ending where the platform rewards rewatches.
- **Vertical micro-drama serial** (ReelShort-class: 60–90-s episodes, 50–100 per season, 9:16): melodrama escalation with a cliffhanger EVERY episode end and a reversal every 3–5 episodes; paywall/subscribe gates sit right after the biggest cliffhangers — dramaturgy and monetization are one system. Staging for 9:16: tight singles, faces huge, one action per frame, verticals in production design; wides barely read. AI note: the highest-volume AI series format — plan like the serialized pole (paper plan for the season, boundaries at cliffs), produce in batches per location; the tight-single grammar plays directly into identity-lock strengths.
- **Faceless VO formats** (explainer, listicle, story-time, doc-style channels): the biggest AI channel class — VO spine + generated B-roll, no on-camera human, no lip-sync anywhere. Structure: hook → promise → segmented payoffs (each segment its own mini-loop) → final payoff + handoff. Production is the doc path (ch. 7b B-roll logic + 23d paper edit) minus interviews.
- **Skit / POV character series:** a recurring character or POV premise in 20–60-s episodic bites — the episodic pole in micro form: character sheet + voice profile amortize, every skit self-contained, the character IS the brand. Formats: recurring-situation escalation, POV-of-X, character-reacts.
- **Part-series (Part 1/2/3):** one short story chopped at its strongest withhold — a serialized micro-arc riding discovery: part 1 must work as pure hook alone, each part re-establishes in one shot (no recap text), the final part pays everything. AI note: generate all parts as ONE production (one continuity context), release chopped.

**Music video / commercial** — concept-repetition-escalation, beat-locked; full defaults live in the Music film and Commercial/Ad baselines (genre-baselines.md) — this file only reminds: these formats may skip narrative entirely; ONE committed visual concept outperforms a compressed plot.

## 23e. Masters' rules (sourced pearls — apply as lints, don't recite)

🟢 **The causation test (Parker/Stone, NYU writing seminar 2014):** take the outline beats — if "and then" fits between two beats, "you're fucked; you've got something pretty boring." Between every pair of beats must fit **"therefore"** or **"but"** (consequence or reversal). Run it mechanically over every outline and treatment at the script stage (production-pipeline ch. 2); an "and then" seam is a cut candidate or a missing turn. Full causal doctrine + the MANDATORY change lint: ch. 23g.

🟢 **The three scene questions (Mamet, memo to The Unit writers):** every scene must answer — **who wants what? · what happens if they don't get it? · why now?** A scene that answers none is information, not drama — and "the audience tunes in for drama, not information": move exposition into conflict or cut it. Use as the scene-level lint after the causation test passes at beat level.

🟢 **Wilder's rules (via Cameron Crowe, Conversations with Wilder) — the five with teeth here:** a clean line of action for the protagonist (one traceable want through the whole film) · **a third-act problem is really a first-act problem** (fix setup, not climax) · the Lubitsch 2+2: let the audience add it up — never show the four (elegance = hidden plot mechanics; ellipsis is also the AI rescue tool, so this pearl pays twice) · the second-act curtain triggers the ending (plant the trigger consciously) · act three builds, then STOPS — don't hang around after the last event. **Plus the VO law:** voice-over must ADD to the image, never describe what the audience already sees — the binding rule for every VO-driven container (mosaic, Scorsese momentum, doc spines).

🟢 **Non-submersible units (Kubrick):** design the film as ~6–8 indestructible sequence blocks — each one so strong it survives on its own — and keep the connective tissue between them minimal. For AI production this is doctrine, not metaphor: it IS sequence-first planning (SKILL rule 2) — invest story and reroll budget in the units, bridge between them with ellipsis.

🟢 **The value turn (McKee, Story):** a scene earns its place only if a value charge FLIPS across it (safe→threatened, hope→despair, together→alone); no flip, no scene. The cutting corollary to Mamet's three questions — run both, they catch different failures.

🟢 **The premise (Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing):** one sentence — "X leads to Y" — that the ENDING proves. Settle it at intake; every container above is only the delivery mechanism for a proven premise, and an unprovable premise is the earliest, cheapest rewrite you will ever get.

(The cutting-room counterpart — Murch's Rule of Six, emotion above all — lives in film-craft §4.)

## 23f. Choosing (and mixing)

1. Intake gate first (23a) — an incomplete story fits no container.
2. Format + genre narrow the field (a 30-s spot never gets a hero's journey; a series gets the sitcom/series shape).
3. Offer max 2 containers with one-line rationale; the user picks — exactly like recipe choice, and usually BEFORE it (structure shapes the shot list more than the look does).
4. Containers mix like recipes: one dominant + one device (three-act + bookend; kishōtenketsu twist inside a sitcom B-plot). More than two = mush.
5. Record the choice in the production bible (style contract section) — structure is canon once approved.

## 23g. The causal chain — doctrine and the mandatory change lint

Why this section is binding: episodic, causally disconnected plotting is the DOCUMENTED native failure mode of LLM writing (EMNLP 2025 survey; arXiv 2311.09648, 2506.02347, 2503.23512 — scaffolding systems exist precisely because raw LLM output adds events instead of causing them). Addition is cheaper than consequence for the model — so the lint below is not optional craft polish, it is the guard against the writer itself. An event without cause is story slop exactly as a guessed beat is prompt slop (SKILL rule 13).

### The doctrine (sourced)
🟢 **Plot IS causality** (Forster, Aspects of the Novel): "The king died and then the queen died" is a story; "the king died, and then the queen died of grief" is a plot — the difference is the whole craft.
🟢 **Propter hoc, not post hoc** (Aristotle, Poetics ch. 9/10): events must be "the consequence, necessary or probable, of the antecedents"; plots "in which the episodes succeed one another without probable or necessary sequence" are "the worst."
🟢 **The chain reaction** (McKee, Story): "motivated actions cause effects that in turn become the causes of yet other effects" — the inciting incident is "the primary cause for all that follows," rippling causally to the climax.
🟢 **Coincidence has a direction** (Coats/Pixar #19; McKee): "Coincidences to get characters into trouble are great; coincidences to get them out of it are cheating." McKee: bring coincidence in early so meaning can build from it, and "never use it to turn an ending. This is deus ex machina, the writer's greatest sin."
🟢 **Deus ex machina** (Aristotle ch. 15): the unraveling "must arise out of the plot itself." A rescue from outside the causal chain erases meaning and emotion. Narrow exception: deliberate, signaled comedy/parody (Life of Brian's spaceship) — the audience must be meant to SEE the device.
🟢 **Plant and payoff, both directions** (Chekhov, letter 1889): "One must never place a loaded rifle on the stage if it isn't going to go off. It's wrong to make promises you don't mean to keep." Every payoff was planted; every prominent plant pays off (payoff economics: film-craft §7).
🟢 **Consequence carries** (Swain, scene/sequel): a scene's outcome becomes a later scene's motivation (goal→conflict→disaster → reaction→dilemma→decision). An outcome nothing reacts to is a dead end.

### The change lint — run on EVERY proposed script/treatment/beat change, before the proposal is offered
A session that proposes a story change answers these eight in its own head first; a change that fails any of them is not offered, it is fixed or dropped. When offering the change, NAME its upstream cause and downstream consequence explicitly — a proposal that cannot state both is not ready.
1. **Therefore/but:** does the new/changed beat connect to its neighbors with "therefore" or "but" — or only "and then"?
2. **Propter hoc:** which prior ON-SCREEN event causes this one? None in the script = episodic addition.
3. **Coincidence direction:** does any coincidence create trouble (allowed, first half) or resolve it (forbidden)?
4. **Ending turn:** is the climax produced by the protagonist's prior choices and ESTABLISHED capabilities — no new character, object, ability, or luck after the midpoint?
5. **Plant/payoff ledger:** everything the change uses was planted; everything it plants will pay off. Flag orphans both ways.
6. **Deletion ripple:** if this beat were deleted, would a later beat stop making sense? No = causally disconnected — justify or cut.
7. **Scene turn + Mamet:** does the scene flip a value AND both advance the plot and stand alone (who wants what · what if they don't · why now)?
8. **Removal rewiring:** when the change REMOVES or alters a beat, every beat that was caused by it is re-wired or flagged — never left orphaned.
